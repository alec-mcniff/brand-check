MMRs, NIL, and the Fight Over “Associated Entity” Status in the House Settlement

Multimedia rights companies have expanded from managing schools’ trademarks and broadcast rights into brokering athlete NIL deals. A federal court will decide whether that makes them “associated entities” under the House settlement.

Alec McNiff · 6 min read

The House settlement reshaped the economics of college athletics when it received final approval in June 2025. Now, less than a year into implementation, the most consequential fight over the settlement’s terms centers on whether multimedia rights companies (“MMRs”), the firms that manage schools’ trademark portfolios, broadcast rights, and sponsorship activations, qualify as “associated entities” when they broker NIL deals for individual athletes. That classification determines whether those deals face regulatory scrutiny from the College Sports Commission (“CSC”) through its NIL Go review platform, or whether they proceed with minimal oversight. In April 2026, class counsel filed a motion asking a federal court to declare that MMRs and third-party brand sponsors are not associated entities. The NCAA and power conferences opposed, arguing that MMRs increasingly function as extensions of schools’ recruiting operations and should face the same fair-market-value scrutiny applied to boosters and collectives. Magistrate Judge Nathanael Cousins held a hearing on May 27 and has not yet ruled, though a second hearing on a related motion to enforce is scheduled for June 10.

Companies like Learfield, Playfly, and JMI Sports have long managed the commercial side of schools’ institutional intellectual property, including things like trademark licensing for stadium signage, broadcast sponsorships, logo placements, and apparel deals. Post-House, these same companies have expanded into sourcing and facilitating NIL deals for individual athletes at some schools where they already hold institutional rights contracts. As a result, a single company can now simultaneously manage a university’s commercial rights program and broker personal endorsement deals for that university’s athletes, sometimes at the direction of the same athletic department staff it already works with. Whether that arrangement makes MMRs “associated entities” under the settlement, and thus subject to CSC oversight, is the question now before the court.

The Settlement’s “Associated Entity” Framework

The House settlement defines five categories of associated entities or individuals. They include entities known to an athletics department to promote or support a specific university’s programs or athletes; individuals who are part of such an entity; individuals who have donated more than $50,000 to a single school; individuals or entities that have been “directed or requested” by a university to assist in recruiting or retaining athletes; and non-publicly traded companies affiliated with any of those categories. The fourth category, the “directed or requested” prong, is the one generating the most attention lately.

Class counsel argue that MMRs are independent, profit-driven companies that serve hundreds of schools and are agnostic to whether one school client performs better than another. In their view, the associated entity definition was designed to capture boosters and NIL collectives—entities with a single-school allegiance whose payments to athletes are functionally recruitment tools. Applying it to MMRs, they contend, sweeps in an entire category of commercial actors that the parties never intended to regulate through the settlement.

The NCAA and power conferences see things differently. In a declaration filed with the court, CSC CEO Bryan Seeley stated that at least some MMR employees are embedded in athletic department offices on campuses, take direction from athletic department staff on which recruits to offer NIL deals to and at what amounts, and are increasingly functioning as extensions of schools’ recruiting operations. On those facts, the NCAA argues, MMRs fit squarely within the fourth category. The NCAA’s position is not that MMRs can never enter NIL agreements with athletes, but that MMR deals that have been “directed or requested” by the university should be subject to the same fair-market-value review that is applied to deals involving boosters and collectives.

The first test of that framework came on May 11, 2026, when an arbitrator ruled in favor of the CSC in a dispute involving Playfly and 18 University of Nebraska football players. The arbitrator found that Playfly qualified as an associated entity in the context of those specific deals, that the deals lacked a valid business purpose because they did not involve goods or services offered to the general public for profit, and that Playfly had engaged in “warehousing” of NIL rights (i.e., paying for the right to use players’ NIL in the future without specifying any immediate deliverables or activations). The deals were reportedly worth $7.5 million combined.

The ruling is the first of its kind in the post-House era, but its precedential value is limited, as arbitration decisions do not bind courts. CSC CEO Seeley himself acknowledged that while the decision is “influential,” he did not consider it a precedent. The broader legal question—whether MMRs are categorically or presumptively associated entities, or whether their status must always be determined through a case-by-case factual inquiry—is for Judge Cousins to decide.

Implications for Brands, Sponsors, and Advertisers

If MMRs are associated entities, every NIL deal they facilitate is subject to CSC review for valid business purpose and fair market value. Brands that have been using MMRs as a one-stop shop for both institutional sponsorships and athlete endorsements would face a new compliance layer on every athlete deal, with longer timelines, additional documentation, and the risk that a deal gets rejected before it can be executed.

If MMRs are not associated entities, brands and MMRs would have a relatively open lane to structure athlete endorsement deals without CSC scrutiny beyond the baseline $600 reporting threshold. But that outcome would not resolve the NCAA’s underlying concern that MMR-facilitated deals could become a pathway for schools to circumvent the settlement’s revenue-sharing cap, and could invite further litigation or federal legislative intervention.

Brands beyond MMRs could also be affected. Class counsel’s motion asked Judge Cousins to declare that third-party brand sponsors do not become associated entities when a school helps connect them with an athlete for a NIL deal. This matters because schools regularly facilitate introductions between sponsors and athletes. An apparel company that already sponsors a university’s athletic program, for example, may want to sign individual players at that school for NIL endorsements, and the athletic department may help make the connection. Under the CSC’s current view, that school involvement could be enough to classify the apparel company as an associated entity, subjecting the deal to NIL Go review for fair market value and valid business purpose. In practice, deals with established for-profit sponsors are probably more likely to survive that scrutiny than deals with boosters or collectives, because a company like Adidas is not going to pay for an endorsement it does not believe is worth the money. But the review process still affects deal timelines and documentation requirements, and the possibility of rejection introduces a layer of uncertainty that would not exist if the sponsor negotiated directly with the athlete without school involvement.

The Protect College Sports Act of 2026, a bipartisan bill introduced by Senators Maria Cantwell and Ted Cruz on May 27, adds another variable. Class counsel Jeffrey Kessler has argued that the bill’s attempt to legislate the classification of MMRs as associated entities actually supports his position, because if the settlement already resolved the question, there would be no need for Congress to address it. Whether that argument gains traction with Judge Cousins remains to be seen, but the bill’s introduction underscores how unsettled this area of the law remains.

The answer to the associated entity question will determine how much friction exists when MMRs broker athlete NIL deals alongside their existing institutional rights contracts. For now, MMRs, schools, athletes, and brands alike are waiting on Judge Cousins to define the boundaries of a system within which all of them will have to operate.

Trading Blows Over Trading Cards: What the NFLPA v. Leaf Lawsuit Reveals About Group Licensing Risks

The NFL Players Association is suing Leaf Trading Cards for allegedly using player likenesses without a group license. The outcome could reshape how the six-player threshold is interpreted across the sports licensing industry.

Alec McNiff · 7 min read

The NFL Players Association and its licensing subsidiary, National Football League Players Incorporated, filed a federal lawsuit on May 1 against Leaf Trading Cards, accusing the company of using the names, images, and likenesses of active NFL players without authorization. The complaint, NFLPA v. Leaf Trading Cards, LLC, No. 3:26-cv-00374-REP (E.D. Va.), brings two claims: false endorsement under Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a), and misappropriation of players’ rights of publicity under Virginia law, Va. Code § 8.01-40 et seq. The NFLPA is seeking a permanent injunction, compensatory and treble damages, disgorgement of Leaf’s profits, and attorneys’ fees, and has demanded a jury trial.

The NFLPA and its licensing arm, NFL Players Inc., serve as the exclusive licensing agents for the commercial use of NFL player attributes when a company uses six or more players’ identities within a 12-month period. That threshold is baked into the NFL Collective Bargaining Agreement. Every player, as a condition of joining the union, assigns his group licensing rights to the NFLPA, which then negotiates deals with companies that want to feature multiple players on products like trading cards, video games, and apparel. The most prominent of those deals is the NFLPA’s recently activated 20-year exclusive with Fanatics, which now controls NFL trading card production under the Topps brand.

Leaf, a Texas-based card company that relaunched under new ownership in 2010, has built its business model around working directly with individual athletes rather than through league-level licenses. Many of its products feature retired players, draft prospects, and celebrities. But the NFLPA’s complaint alleges that Leaf crossed a line by featuring far more than six active NFL players across its football card sets, including Leaf Trinity Football, Leaf Eclectic Signature Series Football, Leaf PayDirt Football, and Leaf Metal Football, all autograph-focused hobby lines sold in retail and collector channels. The complaint also alleges that Leaf failed to secure individual rights from many of the players it featured, even on a non-group basis, and that Leaf’s website and marketing materials prominently feature players’ images and personas.

What counts as group use?

The central legal dispute will likely turn on what triggers the six-player threshold. According to the complaint, Leaf took the position in pre-litigation correspondence that the six-player threshold applies per card or per advertisement, meaning that six or more active players would need to appear on a single card or in a single ad to trigger group licensing obligations. The complaint characterizes that reading as “nonsensical,” arguing that the threshold applies across a product line, so that a trading card set featuring six or more players across its full run triggers the group license requirement regardless of how many appear on any individual card.

If Leaf’s narrower reading were to hold up, it could create a path for companies to avoid group licensing obligations by simply distributing player images across separate cards, pages, or advertisements. That kind of structural workaround would undermine the entire framework that player associations have built to control and monetize collective publicity rights. It is worth noting that the CBA language defines group licensing programs as those involving a total of six or more players “on or in conjunction with products,” which on its face does not limit the count to a single product unit. Whether a court reads that language to support the NFLPA’s product-line interpretation or Leaf’s per-card interpretation could shape how group licensing thresholds are applied industry-wide.

Apart from this interpretation issue, Leaf may raise a First Amendment defense, as it has done unsuccessfully in the past. In certain contexts, courts have held that the use of athletes’ identities can constitute protected speech. The Eighth Circuit reached that conclusion in C.B.C. Distribution & Marketing, Inc. v. Major League Baseball Advanced Media, L.P., 505 F.3d 818 (8th Cir. 2007), where a fantasy sports operator’s use of player names and statistics was found to outweigh the players’ publicity rights. But that case involved publicly available factual data repurposed for a different kind of product, not the commercial reproduction of player likenesses on trading cards sold to collectors. Moreover, in Leaf Trading Cards, LLC v. The Upper Deck Company, No. 3:17-CV-3200-N (N.D. Tex. June 13, 2018), Judge David C. Godbey denied Leaf’s motion to strike Upper Deck’s publicity rights claims under California’s anti-SLAPP statute, which allows defendants to quickly dismiss meritless lawsuits designed to chill First Amendment rights. The court found that Leaf’s trading cards, which featured athletes’ actual photographs, names, and jersey swatches, were not transformative because they derived their value from the athletes’ identities rather than from any creative contribution by Leaf. The court explicitly distinguished Cardtoons, L.C. v. Major League Baseball Players Association, 95 F.3d 959 (10th Cir. 1996), where parody cards featuring caricatures and humorous commentary were found to be protected speech, noting that Leaf’s cards displayed true names and photographs without adding creative elements like humor or social commentary. That ruling suggests Leaf would face an uphill battle making the same argument here, particularly when the complaint alleges that Leaf is using players’ actual photographs and identities on products whose commercial value depends on those identities.

Why this case matters beyond Leaf

This case arrives at a volatile moment in the sports trading card industry. Fanatics now holds exclusive long-term deals with all three major U.S. professional sports leagues and their player associations, making it the sole licensed producer of NFL, NBA, and MLB trading cards. Panini, the former NFL trading card licensee, has its own antitrust lawsuit pending against Fanatics alleging monopolization of the market. Against that backdrop, Leaf’s model of going around league-level licenses and working directly with players is both a business strategy and a legal gamble.

For player associations, this lawsuit is partly about protecting the integrity of the group licensing system itself. If companies can feature dozens of active players across a product line without obtaining a group license, the exclusive deals that fund player benefits and union operations lose their teeth. For trading card companies operating outside the licensed ecosystem, the case is a reminder that the six-player threshold is a real boundary with real consequences, and that creative interpretations of that boundary are likely to be tested in court.

The case is before Judge Robert E. Payne in the Eastern District of Virginia.
